One Trade

My main project. Live since 26 November 2026.

Live, with prices refreshed every weekday JavaScriptPythonyfinanceStatisticsGitHub ActionsCloudflare WorkersAI-assisted (Claude Code)

How many bets does your portfolio really make?

A free browser game that teaches diversification with live market data and pretend money. You build a portfolio with $10,000, guess how many independent bets it makes, then see the answer worked out from real price history. No sign-up, and a round takes a minute or two.

What I found: my own hypotheses mostly didn't hold, and I published that. Once I separated correlation from uneven weighting, correlation cut beginner portfolios' effective bets by only about a quarter, short of the bar I set in advance. And AI stocks did not move closer to the broad market over ten years.

Read more
What you can play
Seven levels, from spotting two holdings that move together to guessing the bets in mystery portfolios. There's also a Sandbox with every measure, a mode for checking your real holdings (nothing is stored or sent), crash replays of 2020, 2022 and the January 2025 AI selloff, a daily "Same bet?" puzzle and a weekly challenge. Returns never earn points.
The question
Does a beginner who mixes a broad index fund, a tech fund and AI stocks hold far fewer independent bets than holdings? And are AI stocks moving more closely with the market than they did ten years ago?
How I tested it
I wrote down the hypotheses, thresholds and random seed and locked them in tagged git commits before downloading a single price, so the tests couldn't be tuned to the results. Every verdict is published, including the ones that went against me.
What the data showed
On the test I first registered, beginner portfolios carried about one-third as many effective bets as holdings, which looked like strong support. But I found that holdings with no correlation at all also passed that test, because uneven weights alone lower the score. So the verdict comes from a cleaner test that measures correlation only, and there the claim was not supported (0.77 against a bar of 0.60). For AI stocks, correlation with the equal-weight S&P 500 fell from 0.53 to 0.47, and the 90% interval includes no change. Dropping any one of the 13 AI stocks changes neither verdict.
What was hard
Getting the statistics right in the browser. The effective-bets measure needs an eigendecomposition, which I check against numpy to nine decimal places; the project has about 1,200 automated tests that run on every push. Harder still was admitting my first test was flawed, and fixing it in dated, public amendments before seeing any data.
How I used AI
I built it with Claude Code as a pair programmer. I wrote the spec, chose the research questions, method and thresholds, and reviewed and tested every change. A public build log records what was built in each session and what I decided or corrected.
What's next
Playtesting with students who have never seen it. A third hypothesis, whether players get better at spotting hidden risk, collects anonymous guesses until 25 January 2027. After that, a classroom link and lesson plan for instructors.

Educational, not investment advice.

Math modeling contest, IMMC 2026, team of four

Guardians of Etosha: protecting wildlife at scale

How a national park with limited staff should split its resources across poaching, wildfire, extinction and ecosystem risks, using Etosha National Park in Namibia as the case study.

OptimizationMATLABPythonGeospatial dataAI-assisted (ChatGPT)
Read more
Why it was hard
Four very different risks had to share one budget and one workforce, across zones of a park with very different geography.
What we did
Built an optimization model for each risk, combined them into one resource mix, allocated it across four zones, and checked it with a staffing plan for 295 people, sensitivity and scenario analysis. Then adapted it to parks in Chile and India.
What I'd improve
We weighted the four risks equally because we found no evidence to rank them. I'd test how much the final allocation depends on that choice.
How we used AI
ChatGPT helped draft framework ideas and MATLAB code, convert the paper to LaTeX, keep notation consistent across sections, and suggest sources. We checked every equation, number and source against original material, and the final model and conclusions are our own. The paper includes a full AI-use report.

Math modeling contest, HiMCM 2025, team of four

Not-So-Super Bowl: the environmental cost of host cities

A model ranking Super Bowl host cities by environmental impact across transportation, energy, water and waste, extended to Formula 1 race locations.

PythonpandasMath modelingSensitivity analysisAI for source finding only
Read more
Why it was hard
The four factors come in different units and from patchy public data, so we had to normalize them and decide how much each should count without simply guessing.
What we did
Min-max normalization, a correlation check between factors, entropy weighting so the data set the weights, and a sensitivity analysis on those weights. Calibrated on Super Bowl LIX in New Orleans.
What I'd improve
With only seven cities, the weights and rankings can shift when a city is added or removed. I'd test that directly, and add uncertainty ranges to the scores.
How we used AI
Only to help find sources, which we then evaluated ourselves. The model and the math are our own.

Independent research, Babson College summer 2025

Persuasive appeals in fast-food advertising

Which persuasive techniques fast-food chains use for healthy versus unhealthy menu items, and which ones drive engagement, from 60 coded Instagram and YouTube ads. Mentored by Dr. Hill Cummings.

Content analysisStatisticsRegressionMarketingAI-assisted analysis (Julius AI)
Read more
What I found
Healthy-item ads leaned harder on how the food looks. For unhealthy items, build-up and music went with fewer likes per follower, while for healthy items, limited-time offers went with more engagement. I closed by designing an ad that uses those two findings.
What I did
Built the coding framework from the advertising literature, collected and coded every ad, and ran chi-square tests, t-tests, correlations and regressions.
What I'd improve
Add a second coder to check reliability, use a larger sample, and correct for running many tests at once, since with this many comparisons a few results can look significant by chance.
How I used AI
Julius AI helped with data exploration, statistical testing and spotting patterns. I designed the study, collected and coded every ad myself, and interpreted the results.

Physics simulation, team of two

3-phase AC induction motor simulation

An interactive model of a stator and squirrel-cage rotor. Sliders change voltage, frequency, rotor resistance, load torque and more, and live graphs show currents, rotor speed, EMF and torque as the motor spins up.

PythonVPythonPhysics
Read more
Why it was hard
The physics chains together: three phase currents make a rotating field, the field induces current in the rotor (Faraday's law), and that current produces torque. Getting slip and net torque to settle correctly took careful time-stepping.
What I did
Built the simulation with a partner for a physics project, with a 3D view, camera controls, pause and reset, and four live graphs.
What I'd improve
Check the simulated torque and slip against a real motor's datasheet.
